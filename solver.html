How does the computer do it?

The numbers

A 3x3 cube can be scrambled into 43,252,003,274,489,856,000 different positions (about 43 quintillion). If you had one cube for every position, they'd cover the Earth about 275 times over.

In 2010, a team of four people (Tomas Rokicki, Herbert Kociemba, Morley Davidson and John Dethridge) proved with lots of help from Google's computers that every position can be solved in 20 moves or fewer. People call that God's Number.

The two-phase algorithm

The computer can't try every possible solution - there are far too many. Instead it uses Herbert Kociemba's two-phase algorithm:

Phase 1: turn the cube until it's in a special "tidy" group where every piece is oriented right and the middle-layer edges are in the middle layer. From there, you can solve it using only U, D, R2, L2, F2 and B2.

Phase 2: solve it using only those moves. Because there are fewer moves to pick from, the search is much smaller.

It uses huge pre-computed tables that say "from here you need at least N more moves", so it can skip any path that's already too long. That trick is called pruning, and it's why it finds a ~20 move solution in milliseconds.

This page uses min2phase, a fast version of the two-phase algorithm by Chen Shuang (who also made csTimer), through the cubing.js library.

Why don't speedcubers solve like this?

The computer's moves look random to a human - there's no pattern to spot, so you can't plan them in your head. CFOP takes more moves but each step is something you can recognise and do from memory, fast. The fastest cubers turn about 10+ moves per second!

There IS a competition event for finding short solutions by hand: Fewest Moves. You get one hour, pen and paper, and the best humans get close to 20.
